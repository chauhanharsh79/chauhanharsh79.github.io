Warehouse Security Guide: Protecting Large Sites with AI

Warehouses are hard to guard: large perimeters, few people, high-value stock. Here is how to design coverage and use AI to actually respond to threats.

Warehouses present a security problem that ordinary CCTV handles poorly. They are large, often sparsely staffed, run around the clock, and hold high-value inventory across a wide perimeter. A wall of monitors that nobody watches is not security — it is evidence collection after a loss. This guide covers how to design warehouse coverage properly and how AI turns passive cameras into an active response system.

Understand the threats first

Warehouse risk is not only the classic night-time break-in. The common loss vectors are:

  • Perimeter intrusion — someone crossing a fence or wall after hours.
  • Internal shrinkage — stock leaving through loading docks or with staff, often in daylight.
  • Loading-bay incidents — the busiest, highest-risk zone for both theft and safety accidents.
  • Unauthorised access to restricted areas — server rooms, high-value cages, hazardous zones.
  • Safety events — a person in a forklift lane or a restricted zone where they should not be.

Good coverage design starts by mapping these against your floor plan, not by counting cameras.

Layered coverage: perimeter, access, interior

Perimeter

The goal at the boundary is early detection — catching someone as they approach or cross, not once they are inside among the stock. Cameras should cover the full fence line with overlapping fields of view so there are no gaps between them. This is exactly where AI matters, because you want an alert the moment a person crosses the line, at night, without a guard staring at a monitor.

Access and loading bays

Every gate, shutter and dock door needs a camera positioned to capture faces and vehicles clearly. Loading bays deserve the highest-resolution cameras you deploy, because this is where most inventory legitimately moves — and therefore where illegitimate movement hides.

Interior and restricted zones

Inside, prioritise aisles with high-value stock, choke points staff must pass through, and any restricted area. Define restricted zones explicitly so the system can alert when someone enters an area they should not be in.

Why AI is the right tool for warehouses

A large site can easily have dozens of cameras. No human can watch them all, and reviewing footage after the fact only tells you what you already lost. AI CCTV changes the model from monitoring to alerting:

  • Person detection on the perimeter — an alert fires when someone crosses a defined boundary, filtering out animals, wind and passing vehicles that plague ordinary motion detection.
  • Restricted-zone rules — trigger only when a person enters a specific area, during specific hours.
  • Instant alerts — the security team is notified in real time, so they can respond while it matters instead of filing a report the next day.
  • Voice warnings — an automatic spoken warning over a speaker tells an intruder they have been seen, which frequently ends the attempt before anything is taken.
  • Faster investigation — when you do need footage, you jump to person events instead of scrubbing hours of empty aisles.

Reducing false alarms at scale

On a large outdoor site, ordinary motion alerts are unusable — every stray dog, gust of wind and vehicle sets them off, and staff quickly ignore all of them. The value of AI here is discipline: alerts fire on people crossing the lines you care about, so when the phone buzzes, it means something. That trust is what makes a warehouse team actually respond.

Practical deployment tips

  • Use higher-resolution cameras (5MP+) at perimeters and loading bays, where identification actually matters.
  • Ensure adequate lighting on the perimeter; even good cameras need something to see. Pair IR or low-light cameras with motion-triggered lighting where possible.
  • Wire the critical cameras for reliability; a dropped wireless feed on a perimeter camera is a blind spot at the worst time.
  • Protect the recorder and network gear with a UPS so an intruder cannot defeat the system by cutting power briefly.
  • Review and adjust detection zones after the first few weeks based on real alert patterns.

The bottom line

Warehouse security is not about having the most cameras — it is about detecting the right events early enough to act. Design coverage in layers from the perimeter inward, put your best cameras where value moves, and use AI to turn a sea of unwatched feeds into a small number of alerts that actually mean something. That is the difference between recording a loss and preventing one.
